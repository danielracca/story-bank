# Story Frameworks

Reference for structuring and delivering a captured story. Choose the frame by the room, not by habit.

---

## Story chemistry

Different stories do different work on a listener. Naming the effect makes selection deliberate instead of accidental. This three-way split is common in stage and speaker training.

### 1. High-stakes
Suspense, risk, plot twist, a hard-won achievement. Something real was on the line and the outcome was not certain.

Produces: focus, attention, memory retention, motivation.

Use when you need to be **believed and remembered** — a proof point, a credibility moment, the answer to "why should we bet on you."

Failure mode: stakes stated but not felt. If the listener does not know what happens if it goes wrong, there are no stakes.

### 2. Vulnerable
Something that cost you. A mistake, a fear, a relationship, a moment you did not have the answer. Told without self-flagellation and with the lesson earned.

Produces: trust, bonding, empathy, generosity.

Use when you need to be **trusted and liked** — building rapport, disarming a sceptic, owning a failure, leading through a hard moment.

Failure mode: fake vulnerability. A "weakness" that is secretly a brag ("I care too much") destroys trust faster than saying nothing. The test: does telling this cost you anything? If not, it is not vulnerable.

### 3. Humorous
Playful, light, feel-good, self-deprecating. Usually short.

Produces: rapport, relaxation, creativity, presence.

Use when you need to **open a room, reset tension, or be human** — the first two minutes of anything, after a hard question, in a hallway.

Failure mode: length. A humorous story that runs long stops being funny. Keep to 30 seconds or fewer.

**Balance rule:** a working bank needs all three. Most people arrive with only high-stakes stories. Actively hunt the vulnerable and humorous ones — they are the ones that make people want to work with you.

## PARA — the default frame

Best for a question that needs answering fast. Most conversational moments.

**Point** — answer in the first sentence. The listener should know your position before you give any context. "The biggest mistake was that we built before we validated."

**Action** — what you did. Decisions, judgement, trade-offs, collaboration. Not a chronological walkthrough.

**Result** — what changed. User outcome, business outcome, team outcome, or a learning. Never stop at "we shipped it."

**Ask** — optional, and usually omitted. Add it when speaking to a decision-maker, closing a pitch, or ending a presentation: the thing you want them to do next.

---

## STAR — the detailed behavioural frame

Use when someone explicitly asks for a full example, or in a structured interview loop that expects it.

**Situation** — 1-2 sentences, no more.
**Task** — what you owned. 1 sentence.
**Action** — the bulk of the answer. Make your individual contribution unmistakable. "I" for your moves, "we" for team results.
**Result** — what changed and what you learned.

STAR's failure mode is front-loading: people spend a minute on Situation and thirty seconds on Action. Invert that.

## Executive frame

For a CEO, a VP, a board, or any senior listener whose time is the constraint.

1. **Recommendation** — the answer, first.
2. **Why** — two or three reasons.
3. **Evidence** — the single strongest signal, not all of them.
4. **Trade-off** — what is being given up. This is what makes you sound senior.
5. **Next step** — what should happen now.

Executive communication is compression. Do the reduction before you speak, not out loud.

---

## Connection frame

For networking, peer conversation, and rapport — where the goal is a relationship, not a verdict.

1. **Hook** — a specific, slightly odd detail that earns the next ten seconds.
2. **Scene** — put them in the room. Present tense works well.
3. **Turn** — the moment it changed.
4. **Land** — the human takeaway, not the professional lesson.
5. **Hand back** — a question that turns the story into a conversation.

The fifth beat is what people forget. A story told at someone is a performance; a story that ends in a question is a connection.

---

## Sales / client frame

For discovery calls, pitches, and objection handling. The story is never about you — it is a mirror.

1. **Someone like them** — "A team about your size, same problem."
2. **The situation before** — their pain, named accurately.
3. **What we did** — brief; this is not a feature list.
4. **The result** — specific and measurable.
5. **The bridge** — "the reason I bring it up is..."

Never name a client without permission. Anonymise by shape: industry, size, stage.

## Delivery mechanics

**Length targets**
- Quick / recruiter question: 30-60 seconds
- Behavioural question: 60-120 seconds
- Strategic question: 45-90 seconds, then let them pull for depth
- Rapport story: 30-45 seconds

Do not answer a five-minute question before it is asked.

**Openings.** Replace "Yeah, so, there was this one time where..." with a launch that already carries the point: "One example was when..." / "The decision I got wrong was..." / "The disagreement was really about..." / "My approach is..."

**Closings.** Land deliberately. "That changed how I approach it today." / "That is why I now test before I build." A story that trails off undoes itself.

**Fillers.** The goal is not zero fillers — it is replacing filler with silence. A one-second pause reads as senior; "um" reads as searching. When an "um" is coming: stop, pause, restart the sentence from the point.

**Sentence discipline.** Keep most spoken sentences under roughly 20 words. When a sentence picks up a second "and," a parenthetical, or a self-correction — stop and start a new one. Complex thinking does not need complex sentences.

**Recovery.** If you lose your place, do not apologise. Reset with a phrase that sounds like structure: "The important part was..." / "What changed the direction was..." / "The lesson I took was..."
